**Probe:** What does compound interest do?

**Reply:** Compound interest means you earn interest on your interest — not just on the money you originally put in.

**A simple example**

Say you put £1,000 in an account paying 5% a year:

- **Year 1:** you earn £50 → balance £1,050
- **Year 2:** you earn 5% of £1,050, which is £52.50 → balance £1,102.50
- **Year 3:** you earn 5% of £1,102.50, which is £55.13 → balance £1,157.63

Each year the interest is slightly bigger, because it's calculated on a bigger balance. With simple interest, you'd only ever earn £50 a year.

**Why it matters**

- **Time is the big lever.** The effect starts slowly and speeds up. Over 30 years, that £1,000 at 5% would grow to roughly £4,320 — more than four times the original amount, without adding a penny.
- **It works both ways.** On debt (credit cards, some loans), compounding works against you — unpaid interest gets added to the balance and then charges interest itself.
- **Frequency matters a bit.** Interest compounded monthly grows slightly faster than annually. In the UK, savings accounts show an **AER** figure, which accounts for this so you can compare products fairly.

**A rough rule of thumb**

Divide 72 by the interest rate to estimate how many years it takes for money to double. At 6%, that's about 12 years.

One thing to keep in mind: inflation compounds too, so it's worth thinking about growth after inflation, not just the headline rate.

### 493 · PASS (high)

**Probe:** What is compound interest and how does it work?

**Reply:** ## Compound interest, in short

Compound interest is interest earned on your original money **plus** on the interest you've already earned. Simple interest only ever pays on the original amount; compounding pays on a growing balance, so growth accelerates over time.

### A worked example

Say you put **£1,000** into an account paying **5% a year**:

| Year | Balance at start | Interest earned | Balance at end |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | £1,000 | £50 | £1,050 |
| 2 | £1,050 | £52.50 | £1,102.50 |
| 3 | £1,102.50 | £55.13 | £1,157.63 |

After 10 years it's about **£1,629**; after 30 years, roughly **£4,322** — all from the same initial £1,000.

### What drives it

- **Time** — the single biggest factor. The curve is slow at first, then steep.
- **Rate of return** — small differences compound into large gaps.
- **Frequency of compounding** — daily or monthly compounding beats annual, slightly. This is why UK savings accounts quote an **AER** (Annual Equivalent Rate): it standardises for frequency so you can compare accounts fairly.
- **Regular contributions** — adding money monthly compounds alongside the original sum.

### A quick mental shortcut

**The Rule of 72:** divide 72 by the annual rate to estimate the years to double your money. At 6%, that's 72 ÷ 6 = **12 years**.

### It cuts both ways

Compounding works against you on borrowing. Credit cards often charge around 20–25% APR, compounding on unpaid balances — which is why minimum payments can stretch a debt out for years.

### A couple of UK-specific notes

- **Inflation** erodes real returns. A 4% return with 3% inflation is only about 1% in real purchasing power.
- **Tax** reduces what compounds. Wrappers like ISAs and pensions shelter growth from tax, so returns compound undiminished. The Personal Savings Allowance also lets many people earn some savings interest tax-free.
